How to choose a coffee maker: the 6 things that matter

01

Start with how you drink coffee

A big pot for the family? Drip. One quick cup before work? A pod machine. Lattes and cappuccinos? Espresso with a steam wand. A slow weekend ritual on a small budget? French press or pour-over. Choosing the brew style first saves you from paying for features you will never use.

💡 Count how many cups your home drinks on a weekday: 1 to 2 points to single-serve, 4 or more points to a drip machine with a carafe.
02

The grinder matters as much as the machine

Coffee loses its aroma quickly once ground. A burr grinder crushes beans to an even size, so the water extracts them evenly; a blade grinder chops them into dust and chunks, which tastes both bitter and sour. For espresso, a fine and consistent grind is essential.

💡 If your budget is tight, a decent burr grinder with a simple brewer often tastes better than an expensive machine with pre-ground coffee.
03

Brew temperature: the hidden spec

Many cheap drip machines never get the water hot enough, which gives weak, flat coffee. The Specialty Coffee Association (SCA) certifies home brewers that hit its brewing standards, including a water temperature of about 197.6 to 204.8°F (92 to 96°C) and a set brew time.

💡 Look for the “SCA Certified Home Brewer” mark when you buy a drip coffee maker: it is an independent lab test, not a marketing badge.
04

Count the real cost per cup

Pod machines are cheap to buy but cost more per cup than ground coffee, every day, for years. Also count filters, descaling solution and, for espresso, a grinder and a milk jug. Over a year, the running costs often matter more than the price of the machine.

💡 Divide the price of a box of pods by the number of pods, then do the same for a bag of beans (about 0.35 oz of coffee per cup): the difference is your yearly cost.
05

Milk drinks? Check the steam wand

If you love lattes, the steam wand decides how good they are. Automatic wands texture the milk for you; manual wands give more control but need practice. A separate milk frother is a cheaper option with a drip or pod machine.

💡 Only buy an espresso machine if you will use it most days: it needs a few minutes of cleaning after each session.
06

Cleaning and descaling

Coffee oils and limescale change the taste over time and can slow the machine down. Check that the carafe, filter basket and drip tray come out easily, and that the brand explains how to descale. Hard tap water means descaling more often.

💡 A thermal carafe keeps coffee hot without a hot plate, which also avoids that burnt taste after 20 minutes.

Coffee makers FAQ

What is the best type of coffee maker for home?

It depends on how you drink coffee. A drip coffee maker is best for several cups at once, a pod machine for one fast cup, an espresso machine for lattes and cappuccinos, and a French press or pour-over for a low-cost, hands-on ritual.

Is a single-serve pod coffee maker worth it?

It is worth it for speed and convenience if you drink one or two cups a day. Pods cost more per cup than ground coffee, so compare the yearly cost before you buy.

What does SCA certified mean for a coffee maker?

The Specialty Coffee Association tests home brewers in its lab and certifies the ones that meet its standards for water temperature, brew time and extraction. It is one of the few independent quality marks for drip coffee makers.

Do I need a grinder for an espresso machine?

For the best espresso, yes: espresso needs a fine, even grind made just before brewing. Some machines include dual-wall filters that work with pre-ground coffee, which is easier but gives less control over taste.

How often should I descale my coffee maker?

Follow the brand’s instructions. As a rule, descale more often if your tap water is hard, and as soon as the machine brews more slowly or the coffee tastes flat.
